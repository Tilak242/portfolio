Investment thesis · India · 2026

AI data centers: investment thesis and market sizing

Type
Thesis, market model, competitor map
Sector
Data-center infrastructure and energy
Output
Word thesis and two Excel sheets

A thesis on where the money is in India's AI data-center build-out. AI racks draw 50 to 150 kW against 10 to 15 kW for older ones, and the grid, cooling and water cannot keep up. I argue the best returns sit in the B2B startups that fix those limits, not in the buildings.

What I built
  • Four startup layers ranked: liquid cooling, battery storage with tariff arbitrage, captive renewable power, and pre-permitted powered land.
  • Financial case for each: liquid cooling adds 40% to 60% to the cooling capex but cuts cooling power by 30% to 40%; taking PUE from 1.55 to 1.20 saves about ₹3.06 crore per MW a year.
  • Valuation view: 15x to 18x EV/EBITDA for air-cooled sites against 22x to 26x for liquid-cooled sites with captive power and storage.
  • Risks and mitigations, and deal structure: performance-linked tranches and service-based revenue models.

Approach

The market model sizes each layer as TAM, SAM and SOM, with every row tied to a stated source or a stated assumption. The competitor sheet maps eight players, from listed cooling makers to bootstrapped startups, by funding, founders and moat.

Investment thesis · Battery-as-a-Service

Battery swapping in India: thesis, sizing and policy

Type
Thesis, market model, competitor map, policy review
Sector
Electric mobility
Output
Doc, two Google Sheets and a policy note

An investment thesis on Battery-as-a-Service, with a bottom-up market model for battery swapping in India, a map of the incumbent players, and a review of the government policies that decide whether the market opens up.

What I built
  • Bottom-up market model: addressable market grows from ₹4,362 crore in 2023 to ₹1,64,000 crore by 2030, built from vehicle sales, pricing and battery-cost data across public sources.
  • Competitive landscape of incumbent leaders: Ola, Hero, Ampere, Ather, TVS and Bajaj.
  • Policy review: the New EV Policy 2024, EMPS 2024, the draft Battery Swapping Policy, FAME-II and the ACC battery incentive scheme (about $2.5B for 50 GWh of cell capacity).

Approach

Policy is the swing factor here. The draft swapping policy proposes battery standardisation, and startups worry that standards favour big players, so I treat policy certainty as a risk in the thesis and not as background.

Research · Single-author study · India

Industrial electricity tariffs, captive power and utility solvency in India

Type
Empirical economics paper
Data
Factory, state-grid and utility records, 2014-15 to 2023-24
Question
Do tariffs, captive power and utility losses feed each other?

A study of whether industrial electricity tariffs, grid reliability, captive self-generation and distribution-utility finances form a reinforcing loop in India, the so-called utility death spiral. I wrote it on my own.

What I built
  • Linked three sources: Annual Survey of Industries factory records (482,371 observations after cleaning), Central Electricity Authority state-grid statistics and Power Finance Corporation utility accounts.
  • Caught a measurement flaw: a factory's unit electricity price is expenditure divided by purchased quantity, so using it to explain purchased quantity builds in a negative relationship. I replaced it with a leave-one-out state-year tariff proxy and treated the plant-level result as a diagnostic only.
  • Found no precise tariff-driven captive response. Peak power deficits do predict higher captive generation: one more percentage point of deficit goes with a 0.0017 higher captive share.
  • Found no significant link between captive share and utility commercial deficits. Subsidy realization and AT&C losses were the more informative correlates.

Approach

The headline is a null result, reported as one. The paper also states its limits: the tariff proxy is not an exogenous regulatory tariff, peak deficit is measured at state level, and the factory model excludes plants that left the grid entirely. The policy point is that supply reliability and utility implementation matter more than tariff design.
